The river begins as a thin stream of melted snow high in the mountains, where the air is cold and the ground is mostly rock. In early spring the water runs clear and fast between the stones, and only a few hardy plants grow along its banks. As the days grow longer, more snow melts, and the stream gathers water from dozens of small channels that join it on the way down. By the time it reaches the first valley, it is wide enough that a person can no longer step across it.

In the valley the slope becomes gentle, and the river slows down. It begins to carry fine sand and silt instead of gravel, and it bends back and forth across the flat ground. Farmers have lived here for centuries, because the soil that the river leaves behind after each flood is rich and easy to work. They grow wheat, barley and vegetables in long narrow fields that run down to the water. Each family keeps a small boat, and in the evenings children fish from the wooden piers that stand at the end of every village.

The oldest village in the valley is built around a stone bridge. Nobody knows exactly when the bridge was made, but the records of the local church mention it more than six hundred years ago. It has five arches, and the middle one is taller than the others so that boats can pass beneath it when the water is high. Every few decades the bridge is repaired, and the masons who do the work are careful to use the same kind of stone that was used at the beginning. Visitors often say that the bridge looks as if it had grown out of the river rather than been built over it.

Below the village the river enters a forest of oak and beech. The trees are tall and close together, and in summer their leaves form a roof so thick that the light on the forest floor is green and dim. Deer come down to the water to drink at dawn, and otters build their dens in the muddy banks. In autumn the leaves turn yellow, orange and red, and many of them fall into the river and float downstream in slow bright rafts. Walkers who follow the path along the bank can hear woodpeckers knocking on the trunks and the occasional splash of a fish jumping for an insect.

After the forest the river reaches the town. The town grew up where two roads crossed the water, and for a long time it was the most important market in the region. Merchants brought wool, salt, iron tools and wine, and they paid a small fee at the gate for the right to sell their goods in the square. The money from those fees paid for the town walls, the clock tower and the covered market hall, all of which still stand today. On market days the square is still crowded, although now most of the stalls sell cheese, bread, fruit and flowers instead of salt and iron.

The town has a small museum that tells the story of the river. One room is filled with old maps that show how the course of the water has changed over the years. In some places the river has moved more than a kilometre from where it used to run, leaving behind curved lakes and marshes where the old channel was. Another room shows the tools that people used to build dikes and canals, from simple wooden spades to the steam pumps that drained the low fields in the nineteenth century. The most popular exhibit is a model of the town as it looked five hundred years ago, with tiny houses, carts and people made by a local craftsman.

Further downstream the land becomes flatter still, and the river is joined by two large tributaries. One of them comes from a region of chalk hills, and its water is pale and cloudy. The other drains a wide area of peat bogs, and its water is dark brown, almost the colour of tea. For several kilometres after they meet, the two colours flow side by side in the same channel without mixing, and from a high place it is possible to see the line between them clearly. Eventually the currents and the wind blend them together, and the river takes on a single grey green colour that it keeps all the way to the sea.

In the lower valley there are larger towns and a few factories. In the past the factories used the river to carry away their waste, and for many years the water was so dirty that almost nothing could live in it. Fifty years ago the people of the region decided that this could not continue. They passed laws that required every factory to clean its water before returning it to the river, and they built treatment plants for the towns as well. The change was slow, but today salmon swim up the river again each autumn, and anglers come from far away to try to catch them.

The last part of the river is an estuary, where fresh water from the land meets salt water from the sea. Twice each day the tide comes in and pushes the sea up the channel, and twice each day it goes out again and leaves wide banks of shining mud. These mud banks are full of worms, snails and small crabs, and they feed huge numbers of birds. In winter tens of thousands of geese and ducks arrive from the north to spend the cold months here. Bird watchers sit in wooden hides along the shore with their telescopes and notebooks, counting the flocks and recording which species they have seen.

At the mouth of the estuary stands a lighthouse. It was built on a rocky island after a ship carrying grain was wrecked there in a storm, and for more than a century a keeper and his family lived in the small house at its base. They kept the lamp burning every night, cleaned the great glass lens and wrote down the weather in a logbook three times a day. The lighthouse is automatic now, and nobody lives on the island, but the logbooks have been kept. Reading them, one can follow the seasons year after year: the calm summer evenings, the autumn gales, the winter fogs, and the first warm days of spring when the snow begins to melt again in the mountains far away and the river starts its long journey once more.
